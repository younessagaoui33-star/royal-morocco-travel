Is It Safe to Travel to Morocco?

The usual scams, travelling alone, food and water, the roads, and what to do if something goes wrong.

The short answer

Yes. Morocco is one of the calmest destinations in North Africa and welcomes millions of visitors every year. Violent crime against tourists is rare. What you are most likely to meet is commercial persistence. It is not danger, it is an annoyance, and it is easy to handle.

Here are the details, plainly.

What actually happens: the usual scams

They are not dangerous, only irritating, and knowing them in advance takes away their effect.

“The tannery is closed, come with me”It is not closed. Someone is taking you to a shop where he earns a commission.
The spontaneous guideHe joins you in the medina, starts explaining things and asks for money at the end. Say a firm “no, thank you” and keep walking.
The taxi without a meterAgree the fare before you get in, every time. If the driver refuses, take another taxi.
Henna in the squareThey take your hand, paint it without asking and then ask for a large sum. Do not offer your hand.
“I will walk you to your riad”At the end he asks for a tip for those two minutes. If you are really lost, step into a shop or a hotel and ask there.

All of this happens mostly in the Marrakech medina and around Jemaa el-Fna, and hardly anywhere else. In the desert, in the Atlas villages and on the roads, you will not meet any of it.

Travelling as a solo woman

It is perfectly possible, and many women do it. In the big cities you will get comments and stares in the street. It is not a physical threat, but it can be tiring. What really helps:

  • Ignore it. Answering only prolongs the conversation.
  • Wear clothes that cover your shoulders and knees. It is not required and nobody will ask you to, but it clearly reduces the attention.
  • At night, stay on streets with people and open shops. The medina at 2 a.m. is empty and disorienting.
  • Sunglasses and headphones. It sounds trivial, and it works.
  • Travel with a private driver, as on our trips. It removes most of this, because you are not looking for transport or negotiating fares in the street.

Food and water

  • Always drink bottled water, even for brushing your teeth the first few days. It is very cheap and saves trouble.
  • No ice from street stalls.
  • Cooked food is safe: tagine and couscous arrive boiling. Be careful with raw salads in very basic places.
  • The orange juice in the square is safe and among the best you will find anywhere.
  • Bring something for your stomach, just in case. New water and new spices catch some people out.

The roads

The roads are better than people expect, and the motorways are good. We do not recommend renting a car to cross the Atlas unless you have experience driving in the mountains: the Tizi n’Tichka is tens of kilometres of tight bends, slow lorries and narrow overtaking spots. Local driving is also more improvised than in Europe.

With a driver it is not only about comfort. He knows the road, which section is under repair and what time the livestock crosses.

Health and documents

  • No vaccines are compulsory to enter Morocco.
  • You need a valid passport. Many nationalities need no visa for tourist stays, so confirm the rule with your consulate.
  • Bring travel insurance. The private clinics in Marrakech are good and charge up front.
  • Keep a photo of your passport on your phone.

The 2023 earthquake

It struck the province of Al Haouz, in the High Atlas south of Marrakech. The mountain villages suffered badly and are still rebuilding. Tourist routes, main roads and the cities have worked normally since then.

If it worries you, tourism is exactly what supports the economy of that region.

What actually ruins a trip to Morocco

In our experience it is almost never safety. It is badly built logistics: a coach tour with 40 people, a “luxury camp” that turns out to be a car park of tents, or a driver in a hurry because another group is waiting.

Choose carefully who you travel with and Morocco is an easy, comfortable destination, the kind people come back to.
